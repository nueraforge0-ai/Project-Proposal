### 1.1 Introduction

Students and staff misplace small but important things on campus every day: phones, identity cards, keys, books, flash disks and chargers. Whether such an item returns to its owner depends largely on whether the owner and the person who found it can be connected. Many universities therefore treat lost property as a recorded service rather than an act of goodwill. At the University of Queensland, for example, people who lose or find something are asked to give Security a description and the place where the item was last seen or found, most items are held for at least two months, and an owner collecting an item must describe it and show photographic identification (University of Queensland, n.d.).

Nkumba University, founded in 1994, has its main campus on Nkumba Hill in Wakiso District along the Kampala-Entebbe Highway, with its academic work organised in seven constituent schools (Nashonji, 2026; Nkumba University, n.d.). The university also has a full-time Security Unit, which marked seven years of service in February 2026 (Nashonji, 2026). From the project team's own experience as students, found items are handed in at the main gate by whoever finds them, and the Security Unit keeps them until someone comes to claim them. A finder who is honest gets the item to the gate, but after that nothing connects the item to its owner. An owner in a hostel or a lecture room can only find out whether a lost item has been handed in by going to the gate and asking. **[CONFIRM: gate procedure, to be validated during requirements gathering]**

Digital lost-and-found systems have already been built for other campuses. Tan and Chong (2023) developed a web and mobile service for the National University of Malaysia in which users post lost or found items with pictures and add security questions to guard against fraud. Murugiah and Mat Nawi (2025) built a web application at Universiti Tun Hussein Onn Malaysia that sends real-time notifications for matched items and gives administrators a dashboard. Ngu (2025) developed an application at Universiti Malaysia Sarawak that uses AI-based image matching. In Uganda, Itaagi (2022) developed a mobile application for finding lost items after research among Makerere University students. These works show that the idea is workable. However, most of the systems we reviewed let users post items themselves on a listing. At Nkumba the item is physically held by the Security Unit, so the digital record should begin with the officer who receives it.

The local environment supports a web-based approach. Smartphones are widely used among university students in Kampala (Aremu et al., 2024), and Wi-Fi masts have been installed in Nkumba University hostels (Nalwanga, 2024). Mobile data is still costly and part of the population remains offline (Daily Monitor, 2026a, 2026b). The system is therefore planned as a lightweight progressive web application (PWA), which can be installed on a phone and keeps working when the connection is weak (BrowserStack, 2026).

The system will use artificial intelligence in three limited ways. It will suggest likely matches between a lost-item report and the register of found items, suggest a category when an item is registered, and summarise records for administrators, for example which categories are lost most often and how long items stay unclaimed. AI models can produce inaccurate statements (Ji et al., 2023). For that reason every suggested match will be confirmed by a Security officer, exact figures will come from database queries rather than from the model, and the design is guided by the human-oversight principles of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023).

Finally, identity cards, phones and similar items carry personal information. Public listings will therefore show only a category and a general description. Identifying details will be used only when ownership is verified at release, in line with the Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019 (Uganda, 2019).
