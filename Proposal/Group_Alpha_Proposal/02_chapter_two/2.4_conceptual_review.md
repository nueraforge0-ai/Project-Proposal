### 2.4 Conceptual Review

#### 2.4.1 Handling of Lost and Found Property in Universities

Universities commonly handle lost property at a staffed point. At the University of Queensland, for example, people report lost or found items to Security with a description and the place of loss or discovery, most items are held for at least two months, and a claimant must describe the item and show photographic identification (University of Queensland, n.d.). Such procedures work well for people who know where to go. Tan and Chong (2023) observe that universities such as Cornell and York provide designated collection points, but argue that these are inefficient when students do not know exactly where to check.

Their survey of 50 students and staff at the National University of Malaysia (UKM) shows the scale of the difficulty in that setting: 66% had lost belongings on campus and 76.5% of them could not recover them, while 62% had found someone's belongings and 68.8% of them could not return the items (Tan & Chong, 2023). Many respondents said they would post on social media (56%) or tell a nearby office (28%). These figures describe UKM and cannot be assumed for Nkumba University. They do show why the team will measure the situation at Nkumba before designing the system (Objective 1).

#### 2.4.2 Digital Lost and Found Systems

Digital systems replace posting and asking with a searchable record. Tan and Chong (2023) developed FoundeLost for UKM, a web and Android application in which users log in with a university email and matric number, post lost or found items with pictures, search by keyword and category, and answer security questions before an item is released. Items older than a month are deleted automatically. Murugiah and Mat Nawi (2025) developed FindItHere at Universiti Tun Hussein Onn Malaysia, which matches lost and found reports, sends real-time notifications and gives administrators a dashboard. Ngu (2025) developed FindIt at Universiti Malaysia Sarawak, a mobile application with AI-based image matching, notifications and status tracking, supported by an administrator web interface for claims and accounts. A commercial service, Troov, offers an online matching algorithm, notifications and a security question, but was designed for use worldwide and not around one campus (Tan & Chong, 2023).

Closer to Nkumba, Twayigize (2025) proposed an AI-driven lost and found management system at Strathmore University in Kenya, using the CLIP image-text model to match lost and found items from user reports with descriptions, categories, locations, dates and images. In Uganda, Itaagi (2022) developed a mobile application for finding lost items, based on research among Makerere University students. Taken together, these works show that digital lost-and-found systems are feasible on campuses. In most of them, however, users post items themselves and arrange the handover among themselves, which is a different arrangement from a university where one office holds the items.

#### 2.4.3 Verification of Ownership and Handling of Personal Items

A lost-and-found system must prevent the wrong person from collecting an item. Tan and Chong (2023) report that students at UKM often could not determine the actual owner of an item, which raised the chance of the wrong person collecting it. FoundeLost and Troov respond with security questions, which the finder or the system sets and the claimant answers (Tan & Chong, 2023). In a custody model, the officer holding the item can ask a claimant for details that only the owner would know and compare them with the record before releasing the item, which is also how the University of Queensland process works (University of Queensland, n.d.).

Found items often carry personal information, for example identity cards and phones. Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019 provides the legal context, and this study applies its principles of purpose limitation, data minimisation, controlled access and appropriate retention (Uganda, 2019). In practice, public listings show only a category and a general description, and identifying details are used only for verification at release.

#### 2.4.4 Matching and Artificial Intelligence in Lost and Found

Matching lost reports with found items is the step where AI can save the most effort. Text similarity can be calculated from sentence embeddings, which represent the meaning of a description as numbers that can be compared (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). Image-text models such as CLIP can compare a photograph with a written description (Radford et al., 2021), and Twayigize (2025) uses CLIP for this purpose in a lost-and-found setting. Ngu (2025) likewise reports image matching in FindIt.

AI output can also be wrong. Language models can generate fluent but inaccurate content (Ji et al., 2023) and can overgeneralise when summarising (Peters & Chin-Yee, 2025). Responsible use therefore requires identified risks to be managed with human oversight (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023). In the proposed system, matches are ranked suggestions for an officer, summaries are written from figures calculated by database queries, and nothing is released without a person's decision.

#### 2.4.5 System Security, Privacy and Access Control

A lost-and-found platform holds contact details, photographs and descriptions of personal belongings. Recent work on access control emphasises that sensitive records should be reached through authentication and role-based privileges (Rangappa, 2023). Students should therefore see only their own reports and claims, Security Officers should see what their duties require, and Administrators should have a separate level of access. Common web risks will be checked against the OWASP Top 10 (OWASP Foundation, 2021), and Privacy by Design is used as guidance (International Organization for Standardization, 2023).

#### 2.4.6 Usability, Accessibility and Mobile Access

A system is only useful if people use it. Tan and Chong (2023) list limited user engagement as a challenge and report that testers wanted pop-up notifications and guidance for first-time users. Smartphones are widely used among university students in Kampala (Aremu et al., 2024), national connectivity is growing modestly (Olupot, 2025), and data costs and offline populations remain barriers (Daily Monitor, 2026a, 2026b). A lightweight progressive web application can be installed from a browser, works on any device with a modern browser and supports limited connectivity (BrowserStack, 2026). Tan and Chong (2023) note that their mobile application ran only on Android, which limits access. A PWA avoids that limitation. Usability will be measured with the System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996; Wafa et al., 2024).
